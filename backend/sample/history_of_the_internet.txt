A Brief History of the Internet

The story of the internet begins in the late 1960s, deep inside the United States Department of Defense. In 1969, a research project called ARPANET (Advanced Research Projects Agency Network) successfully sent the first message between two computers located at UCLA and the Stanford Research Institute. The message was supposed to be "login," but the system crashed after just two letters — making "lo" the first word ever transmitted over what would eventually become the internet.

Throughout the 1970s, engineers worked to develop the foundational protocols that would allow different computer networks to communicate with one another. In 1974, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published a landmark paper describing TCP (Transmission Control Protocol), the technology that made it possible for data to be broken into packets, routed independently across a network, and reassembled correctly at the destination. This idea of "packet switching" was revolutionary and remains at the core of how the internet works today.

The 1980s saw the internet grow beyond military and academic circles. The domain name system (DNS) was introduced in 1983, replacing the cumbersome practice of maintaining a single master list of hostnames. This allowed the network to scale gracefully as thousands of institutions joined. By 1986, the NSFNET (National Science Foundation Network) created a high-speed backbone connecting universities across the United States, dramatically accelerating the spread of networked computing.

Perhaps the single most transformative moment in internet history came in 1991, when Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working at CERN in Switzerland, publicly released the World Wide Web. Berners-Lee invented HTML (HyperText Markup Language), HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol), and the URL (Uniform Resource Locator) — together, these technologies made it possible for anyone to publish and navigate interconnected documents through a simple browser. The web was not the internet itself, but it gave ordinary people an accessible and intuitive interface to it.

The mid-1990s brought an explosion of public interest. Netscape Navigator launched in 1994 and made web browsing mainstream. Companies scrambled to establish an online presence. The period known as the "dot-com boom" saw enormous investment in internet-based businesses, many of which promised to transform entire industries. When the bubble burst in 2000–2001, hundreds of companies collapsed, but the infrastructure and cultural shift they had sparked remained.

Broadband connections replaced dial-up in the early 2000s, making the internet faster and always-on for home users. This enabled a new wave of services built around user-generated content. Wikipedia launched in 2001. Friendster, MySpace, and eventually Facebook redefined social interaction. YouTube (founded 2005) made video sharing trivial. Twitter distilled public discourse into 140 characters. Smartphones — especially after the iPhone's debut in 2007 — put the internet in billions of pockets and fundamentally changed how people consume information.

Today the internet connects more than five billion people and underpins virtually every sector of the global economy — from banking and healthcare to education and entertainment. Cloud computing allows organisations of any size to rent vast computational resources on demand. Artificial intelligence, trained on data gathered from across the web, is reshaping what computers can do. And yet, the core ideas that made it all possible — open standards, packet switching, and the humble hyperlink — are largely unchanged from their origins decades ago.

The internet's history is, at its heart, a story about collaboration: researchers sharing protocols freely, engineers building on one another's work, and billions of users contributing content that makes the whole system more valuable. It is also a story that is very far from over.
